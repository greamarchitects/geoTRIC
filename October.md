## Diagnostic assessment, treatment and clinical course

Ms Çalliku was admitted because of psychotic symptoms. The initial admission to the open FRITZ ward was voluntary. FRITZ provides an intensive, multimodal inpatient, day-clinic and outpatient treatment programme specifically tailored to the needs of young people experiencing their first psychotic crises.

The patient was soon able to participate in the ward’s therapeutic programme, which consisted of specific individual and group psychotherapy, psychoeducation, peer counselling, metacognitive and cognitive training, physical activation, occupational therapy and music therapy. In addition, Ms C. received support from a job coach through our Individual Placement and Support programme, as well as counselling from a social worker.

Initially, she displayed a psychotic clinical picture consisting of auditory hallucinations—some of them commanding—as well as disorganisation and partial disorientation. Olanzapine was restarted and gradually increased. Despite antipsychotic medication, her condition deteriorated.

The patient developed increasingly paranoid fears and exhibited unsafe behaviour, including carrying a knife. She also developed negative symptoms consisting of anhedonia, flattened affect and increasing mutism. She answered questions by nodding or shaking her head. A high level of underlying tension was observed. The patient confirmed experiencing threats and persecution.

A risk to herself developed in the form of a tendency to run away and refusal to consume food and fluids.

For these reasons, she was transferred to the locked/protected Ward 32. Because of the aforementioned tendency to run away and the risk to herself in the context of disorganisation and reported auditory hallucinations associated with her psychotic experiences, she was detained under the Berlin Mental Health Act (**PsychKG**). This detention remained in effect until 13 November 2025.

There continued to be no improvement in her symptoms after commencing antipsychotic treatment with olanzapine and amisulpride.

A comprehensive physical diagnostic assessment was therefore performed because an autoimmune encephalopathic cause of the symptoms was suspected.

A cranial MRI produced unremarkable findings. Autoimmune testing of the cerebrospinal fluid was negative.

A sample was sent to the specialised laboratory of Professor Prüß’s research group at Charité. Indirect immunofluorescence testing on unfixed mouse brain tissue demonstrated stained ependymal-cell nuclei and a distinctly punctate IgG-binding pattern around Purkinje cells. Further testing for additional specific antibodies was recommended and performed by Labor Stöcker. Testing for AGNA IgG, anti-KCNA2 IgG and the anti-cerebellar IgG panel produced negative results.

Based on the clinical observations, the progression of the symptoms and the lack of response to antipsychotic medication, the suspicion of autoimmune encephalitis became stronger despite the absence of positive specific antibodies. Treatment was therefore initiated with intravenous high-dose corticosteroid pulse therapy, followed by gradual oral tapering.

The symptoms subsequently regressed and ultimately ceased. Complete remission of the psychotic symptoms can be assumed.
